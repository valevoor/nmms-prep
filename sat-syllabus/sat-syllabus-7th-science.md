### `sci-7-01-ever-evolving-science` — Chapter 1: The Ever-Evolving World of Science
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 1 | Chapter 1 | Book pp. 1–6 (PDF pp. 17–22) | **Sub-Discipline:** Scientific Inquiry
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Nature of Scientific Inquiry, Questioning and Observation, Interconnectedness of Science Disciplines
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **1.1 Science as a Process of Thinking and Doing:**
    - Science is not a static compilation of facts; it is an ongoing process of observing, asking questions, performing hands-on experiments, and discovering how the natural world works.
    - **Responsibility & Sustainability:** Understanding how human activities are linked to the natural world and how science addresses environmental challenges.
    - **Interconnectedness of Scientific Fields:** Discoveries in physics, chemistry, biology, and earth sciences are deeply connected (e.g., properties of materials $\to$ chemical and physical changes $\to$ heat flow and the water cycle $\to$ life processes in plants and animals $\to$ timekeeping, light, shadows, and planetary motions).
    - **Activity 1.1 ("Question the Answer"):** Reversing conventional test-taking by formulating creative scientific questions for a given answer ("Just make it half", "Just add some milk", "42"), cultivating divergent scientific thinking.
- **Key Terms & Clues:**
  - *Process of discovery*, *Butterfly and paper plane page-number motifs* (early flight inspired by observing bird wings and paper planes), *Question the Answer*, *non-evaluative introductory chapter*.
- **Mapping & Disambiguation Rules:**
  - **Map here** ONLY if a question explicitly references the introductory themes of Class 7 Chapter 1 ("Question the Answer" activity or the butterfly/paper-plane motif).

### `sci-7-02-acids-bases-neutral` — Chapter 2: Exploring Substances: Acidic, Basic, and Neutral
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 1 | Chapter 2 | Book pp. 7–22 (PDF pp. 23–38) | **Sub-Discipline:** Chemistry (Acids, Bases, Indicators, & Salts)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Acids, Bases and Salts, Natural Acid-Base Indicators (Litmus, Turmeric, Red Rose, Hibiscus), Olfactory Indicators, Neutralisation in Everyday Life
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **2.1 Nature of Common Substances & Acid-Base Indicators:**
    - **Acid-Base Indicators:** Substances (natural or synthetic) that show different colours (or odours) in acidic and basic solutions.
    - **2.1.1 Litmus as an Indicator (Activities 2.1 & 2.2):**
      - **Source of Litmus:** A natural indicator extracted from **lichens** (organisms formed by the symbiotic association of a **fungus and an alga**, growing on rocks and trees in regions with abundant rainfall and clean air). Available as red and blue litmus paper strips or solution.
      - **Preparation of Lime Water:** Prepared by mixing **lime (*chuna*, calcium oxide)** in water, leaving it undisturbed for an hour, and filtering the clear liquid (**solution of calcium hydroxide in water**). *(Not to be confused with lime fruit!)*
      - **Three-Group Classification of Household Samples (Tables 2.1 & 2.2):**

        | Group | Effect on Litmus Paper | Nature | Textbook Examples |
        | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
        | **Group A** | Turns **Blue litmus $\to$ Red** (Red litmus unchanged) | **Acidic** | Lemon juice, *amla* juice, tamarind water, vinegar, curd, orange juice, grape juice |
        | **Group B** | Turns **Red litmus $\to$ Blue** (Blue litmus unchanged) | **Basic** | Soap solution, baking soda solution, lime water, washing powder solution |
        | **Group C** | **No change** in either blue or red litmus | **Neutral** | Tap water, sugar solution, common salt solution |

      - **Physical Properties & Common Edible Acids (Fig. 2.4):**
        - **Acids:** Generally taste **sour**.
          - **Lemon / Orange:** **Citric acid**
          - ***Amla* (Indian Gooseberry):** **Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)** and **Citric acid**
          - **Tamarind (*Imli*) / Unripe Grapes:** **Tartaric acid**
          - **Tomato:** **Citric acid** and **Oxalic acid**
          - **Curd (*Dahi*):** **Lactic acid**
          - **Vinegar:** **Acetic acid**
          - **Ant sting / bite:** **Formic acid**
        - **Bases:** Generally taste **bitter** and feel **soapy or slippery** to touch (when rubbing baking soda solution between fingers). *(Caution: Not everything that tastes bitter is a base — e.g., **bitter gourd [*karela*]** tastes bitter but is **not** basic in nature).*
    - **2.1.2 Red Rose (and Other Plant Extracts) as an Indicator (Activities 2.3 & 2.4):**
      - Prepared by crushing fallen red rose petals with a mortar and pestle, immersing in hot water for 5–10 minutes, and filtering the coloured extract.
      - **Colour Change:** Turns a **shade of red** in an **acidic solution** and a **shade of green** in a **basic solution** (unchanged in neutral solutions).
      - **Other Natural Indicators:** Extracts of **beetroot**, **purple/red cabbage**, **Indian blackberry (*jamun*)**, and **red hibiscus (*gudhal*)**.
      - **Hydrangea Flowers & Soil pH (*Dive Deeper*):** *Hydrangea* plants (grown in the Himalayas and North-Eastern states) produce **blue flowers in acidic soil** and **pink or red flowers in basic soil**.
    - **2.1.3 Turmeric (*Haldi*) as an Indicator (Activity 2.5):**
      - Yellow turmeric paper turns **red (reddish-brown) in basic solutions** (why a yellow curry stain on a white shirt turns red when washed with soap, and how secret messages are written with soap solution on turmeric paper or sprayed on *National Science Day*!).
      - Remains **yellow in both acidic and neutral solutions** (hence turmeric paper **cannot differentiate between acidic and neutral solutions** on its own, unless paired with a known base first).
    - **2.1.4 Olfactory Indicators (Activity 2.6):**
      - Substances whose **odour (smell) changes** in an acidic or basic medium — e.g., cloth/paper strips steeped overnight with finely chopped **onions** (retain odour in acid like tamarind water, lose characteristic odour in base like baking soda solution; useful for **visually impaired / blindfolded** testing).
  - **2.2 What Happens When Acidic Substances Mix with Basic Substances? (Neutralisation — Activity 2.7):**
    - When an acid solution (lemon juice + blue litmus $\to$ red) is mixed with a base solution (lime water) in sufficient quantity, they cancel each other's effects so the resulting solution is neither acidic nor basic.
    - **Neutralisation Reaction:** A chemical reaction between an acid and a base that forms **salt and water** with the **evolution (release) of heat**:
      $$\text{Acid} + \text{Base} \to \text{Salt} + \text{Water} + \text{Heat}$$
  - **2.3 Neutralisation in Daily Life:**
    1. **Treatment of Red Ant Bite (Situation 1):** A red ant bite injects an acidic liquid (**formic acid**) into the skin, causing redness and stinging pain; neutralised by rubbing moist **baking soda** (a mild base).
    2. **Soil Treatment in Agriculture (Situation 2):**
       - **Too Acidic Soil** (often caused by excessive use of chemical fertilisers) $\to$ treated with **lime** (calcium oxide / calcium hydroxide, which is basic).
       - **Too Basic Soil** $\to$ treated by adding **organic matter (manure or composted leaves)**, which releases acids in the soil to neutralise excess base.
    3. **Treatment of Factory Waste (Situation 3):** Acidic industrial effluents discharged into lakes/rivers kill fish and aquatic life; therefore, factory waste is **neutralised by adding basic substances** before being released into water bodies.
- **Key Terms, Scientists & Heritage Boxes:**
  - *Acid-base indicator*, *litmus*, *lichens (fungus + alga)*, *lime water (calcium hydroxide from chuna/calcium oxide)*, *citric acid*, *ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)*, *tartaric acid*, *oxalic acid*, *lactic acid*, *acetic acid*, *formic acid*, *red rose extract (red in acid, green in base)*, *turmeric paper (red in base, yellow in acid/neutral)*, *hydrangea (blue in acidic soil, pink/red in basic soil)*, *olfactory indicator (onion)*, *neutralisation reaction*, *Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray* (Father of Modern Indian Chemistry; founded **Bengal Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals** in 1901; wrote on the history of chemistry in India; advocated mother tongue instruction).
- **Mapping & Disambiguation Rules:**
  - **Map here** for all questions on acids, bases, neutral substances, natural indicators (litmus, turmeric, red rose, hibiscus, purple cabbage, onion olfactory indicator), edible organic acids, and everyday neutralisation (ant bite, acidic/basic soil treatment, factory waste).
  - **Disambiguation with `sci-7-04-metals-non-metals`:** If a question specifically tests the **acidic/basic nature of metal oxides ($\text{MgO}$) vs. non-metal oxides ($\text{SO}_2$)** using litmus paper, map to `sci-7-04-metals-non-metals`.

### `sci-7-03-electricity-circuits` — Chapter 3: Electricity: Circuits and their Components
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 1 | Chapter 3 | Book pp. 23–40 (PDF pp. 39–56) | **Sub-Discipline:** Physics (Current Electricity & Circuits)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Electric Current and Circuit Components, Cell vs. Battery, Incandescent Bulb vs. LED Polarity, Switch (Open and Closed Circuit), Circuit Diagrams and Symbols, Electrical Conductors and Insulators
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **3.1 & 3.2 A Torchlight & Simple Electrical Circuits:**
    - **3.2.1 Electric Cell:** A compact portable source of electrical energy having **two terminals**:
      - **Positive ($+\text{ve}$) terminal:** Small protruding **metal cap** on one side.
      - **Negative ($-\text{ve}$) terminal:** Flat **metal disc** on the opposite side (placed towards the spring side of a cell holder).
    - **3.2.2 Battery (Activity 3.3):**
      - A combination of **two or more cells** connected such that the **positive ($+$) terminal of one cell is connected to the negative ($-$) terminal of the next cell** (either end-to-end in a torch or side-by-side in a compartment linked by a thick metal strip/wire).
      - Connecting multiple cells provides electrical energy for a longer time and/or greater energy.
    - **3.2.3 Electric Lamp (Activities 3.4 & 3.5):**
      1. **Incandescent Lamp (Bulb):** Consists of a glass bulb enclosing a thin wire called the **filament** (which gets hot and glows to produce light when current passes through it). The filament is supported by two thicker wires connected to the lamp's two terminals — the **metal case** around the base and the **metal tip** at the bottom centre (separated by an insulator). A **"fused" bulb** does not glow because its **filament is broken**, creating an open circuit.
      2. **Light Emitting Diode (LED):** Modern lamp with **no filament**; has two leads/wires of unequal length:
         - **Longer wire** $=$ **Positive ($+$) terminal**.
         - **Shorter wire** $=$ **Negative ($-$) terminal**.
    - **3.2.4 Making an Electric Lamp Glow — Electrical Circuit (Activities 3.6 & 3.7):**
      - **Electrical Circuit:** A complete, closed conducting path between the positive and negative terminals of a cell/battery through which electric current flows.
      - **Direction of Electric Current:** Conventionally taken to be from the **positive ($+$) terminal to the negative ($-$) terminal** of the electric cell.
      - **Incandescent Bulb vs. LED Connection Rule:**
        - An **incandescent lamp** glows regardless of which terminal is connected to the $+$ or $-$ terminal of the cell, as long as the circuit is complete.
        - An **LED allows electric current to pass in ONE direction only**: it glows **ONLY** when its **positive terminal (longer wire)** is connected to the **positive terminal of the battery** and its **negative terminal (shorter wire)** is connected to the **negative terminal of the battery** (reversing the connections prevents it from glowing).
    - **3.2.5 Electric Switch (Activities 3.8 & 3.9):**
      - A simple device (demonstrated using a rotatable safety pin and two drawing pins on cardboard) that either **completes or breaks** an electrical circuit; can be placed **anywhere** in the circuit.
      - **ON Position $\to$ Closed Circuit:** Safety pin touches both drawing pins, closing the gap so current flows and the lamp glows.
      - **OFF Position $\to$ Open Circuit:** An **air gap** between the terminals prevents current flow, and the lamp does not glow.
  - **3.3 Circuit Diagrams (Table 3.2 & Activity 3.10):**
    - A representation of an electrical circuit using standard symbols for its components:
      - **Electric cell:** A **long thin vertical line ($+$ terminal)** parallel to a **shorter, thicker vertical line ($-$ terminal)**.
      - **Battery:** Two or more cell symbols joined in series ($+|- \dots +|-$).
      - **Electric lamp (bulb):** Circle with a looped filament symbol inside ($\otimes$).
      - **LED:** Triangle pointing towards a vertical bar with two small arrows pointing outward (indicating light emission); the **triangle points in the direction of current flow (from $+$ to $-$)**.
      - **Switch in 'ON' position:** Closed contact line between two terminals.
      - **Switch in 'OFF' position:** Open tilted contact line between two terminals.
      - **Connecting wire:** Straight line.
    - **International Standards Organisations (*Dive Deeper*):** **IEC** (International Electrotechnical Commission), **ANSI** (American National Standards Institute), and **IEEE** (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers).
  - **3.4 Electrical Conductors and Insulators (Activity 3.11 & Table 3.3):**
    - **Conduction Tester:** A simple circuit with a cell, lamp, and two free wire ends used to test whether materials allow current to pass.
    - **Conductors (Good Conductors of Electricity):** Materials through which electric current flows easily — **all metals** (copper, aluminium, iron, silver, gold — e.g., metal spoon, coin, key, sewing needle, pin, aluminium foil), **pencil lead (graphite)**, and the **human body**.
      - **Best Conductors (*Dive Deeper*):** **Silver, copper, and gold** are the best electrical conductors; **copper** is used for household wiring due to its lower cost and abundant supply.
    - **Insulators (Poor Conductors of Electricity):** Materials through which current cannot pass — **plastic** (scale, wire covering), **rubber** (eraser, gloves), **wood** (dry stick/block), **glass** (bangle), **paper**, **wax** (candle), **cork**, and **ceramics**.
    - **Complementary Roles & Electrical Safety:** Conductors form wires, plug pins, switches, and sockets, while insulators cover wires, plug tops, and switchboards to protect users from fatal electric shocks (never touch switches with wet hands or use damaged insulation).
    - **DC vs. AC (*Dive Deeper*):** Cells and batteries supply **Direct Current (DC)** for small portable devices, whereas power plants (like the **Bhakra Nangal hydroelectric dam** on the Sutlej river, thermal, or solar plants) supply **Alternating Current (AC)** to wall sockets to run heavy appliances.
- **Key Terms & Symbols:**
  - *Electric cell*, *positive terminal (metal cap, long line)*, *negative terminal (metal disc, short thick line)*, *battery*, *incandescent lamp*, *filament*, *fused bulb*, *Light Emitting Diode (LED — longer wire $+$, shorter wire $-$)*, *electrical circuit*, *closed circuit (ON)*, *open circuit (OFF)*, *circuit diagram*, *IEC / ANSI / IEEE*, *conductors*, *insulators*, *Direct Current (DC)*, *Alternating Current (AC)*, *button cells*, *Bhakra Nangal Dam*.
- **Mapping & Disambiguation Rules:**
  - **Map here** for basic circuit components, cell/battery terminal connections, incandescent bulb filament, LED wire-length polarity, open vs. closed switches, circuit diagram symbols, and testing electrical conductors vs. insulators.
  - **Disambiguation with Class 8 Chapter 4 (`sci-8-04-electricity-effects`):** If a question tests the **magnetic effect of current (Oersted's compass deflection, electromagnets)**, the **heating effect of current (nichrome heating element, fuse)**, or the **chemical structure of a Voltaic cell / lemon cell / dry cell (zinc container & carbon rod)**, map to `sci-8-04-electricity-effects`.

### `sci-7-04-metals-non-metals` — Chapter 4: The World of Metals and Non-metals
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 1 | Chapter 4 | Book pp. 41–56 (PDF pp. 57–72) | **Sub-Discipline:** Chemistry (Materials & Elements)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Metals and Non-Metals, Physical Properties (Lustre, Malleability, Ductility, Sonority, Conductivity), Chemical Properties (Oxides of Metals vs. Non-Metals), Rusting of Iron and Corrosion, Uses of Metals and Non-Metals
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **4.1 Physical Properties of Metals vs. Non-Metals (Activities 4.1–4.4):**

    | Property | Metals (e.g., Iron, Copper, Aluminium, Gold, Silver) | Non-Metals (e.g., Coal/Carbon, Sulfur [*Gandhak*], Oxygen, Phosphorus) |
    | :--- | :--- | :--- |
    | **Appearance** | **Lustrous** (shiny **metallic lustre** when freshly cut/polished) | **Non-lustrous** (dull appearance) |
    | **Hardness & State** | Generally **hard solids** at room temperature.<br>**Exceptions:** **Sodium ($\text{Na}$)** and **Potassium ($\text{K}$)** are **soft enough to be cut with a knife**; **Mercury ($\text{Hg}$)** is a **liquid at room temperature** (used in thermometers). | Generally soft solids (sulfur, phosphorus) or gases (oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, chlorine). |
    | **Malleability (Beating with Hammer)** | **Malleable** — flatten into thin sheets without breaking (e.g., **silver foil** on sweets, **aluminium foil** for wrapping food, iron tools shaped red-hot by ironsmiths). **Gold and silver are the most malleable metals.** | **Non-malleable / Brittle** — solid non-metals (coal, sulfur) break into powdery pieces when hammered (**brittle**). *(Note: **Wood** neither flattens nor shatters into powder — it is **neither malleable nor brittle**).* |
    | **Ductility (Drawing into Wires)** | **Ductile** — can be drawn into thin wires (electrical wires of copper/aluminium, ornaments, stringed musical instruments like *veena, sitar, violin, guitar*, tea strainer, **steel** wire ropes [iron + carbon] in suspension bridges and cranes). **Gold is so ductile that $1\text{ g}$ can be drawn into a $2\text{ km}$ long wire!** | **Non-ductile** — cannot be drawn into wires. |
    | **Sonority (Dropping / Striking)** | **Sonorous** — produce a deep **ringing sound** when struck (school bells, *ghungroos*, metal coins/spoons). | **Non-sonorous** — produce a dull thud sound. |
    | **Conduction of Heat** | **Good conductors of heat** (metal cooking vessels, *tawas*, pans). | **Poor conductors (insulators) of heat** (wood/plastic handles of cooking pans). |
    | **Conduction of Electricity** | **Good conductors of electricity** (aluminium foil, iron nail, copper wire make tester bulb glow). | **Poor conductors of electricity** (sulfur, coal, dry wood, stone, rubber, nylon). |

  - **4.2 Effect of Air and Water on Metals: Rusting of Iron & Corrosion (Activity 4.5):**
    - **Three-Bottle Rusting Experiment (Fig. 4.4 & Table 4.3):**
      - **Bottle A (Dry Air Only):** Iron nail + **silica gel** (absorbs moisture to keep air dry), tightly stoppered $\to$ **NO rusting**.
      - **Bottle B (Water Only, No Air):** Iron nail submerged in **freshly boiled and cooled water** (dissolved air removed) covered with a **layer of oil** (prevents air from dissolving) $\to$ **NO rusting**.
      - **Bottle C (Both Air and Water):** Iron nail partially dipped in water in an open bottle (exposed to both air and water) $\to$ **Brown flaky deposit (rust) forms!**
    - **Conclusion:** Presence of **BOTH air (oxygen) and water (moisture)** (i.e., **moist air**) is essential for the **rusting of iron**.
    - **Corrosion of Other Metals:** Gradual deterioration of metal surfaces by air, water, or chemicals is called **corrosion**:
      - **Iron:** **Brown** coating (rust / iron oxide).
      - **Copper:** **Green** coating on surface.
      - **Silver:** **Black** coating on surface.
    - **Prevention of Rusting:** **Painting**, **oiling**, **greasing**, and **galvanisation** (depositing a protective thin layer of **zinc metal** over iron).
    - **The Iron Pillar of Delhi (*Fascinating Facts*):** Built during the reign of **Chandragupta II** more than **1600 years ago** ($\approx 8\text{ metres}$ high, weighs $> 6000\text{ kg}$); has barely any rust despite centuries of wind, rain, and heat, showcasing ancient Indian metallurgical mastery.
  - **4.3 & 4.4 Chemical Nature of Metal Oxides vs. Non-Metal Oxides (Activities 4.6–4.8):**
    - **Reaction of Metals with Oxygen (Basic Oxides — Activity 4.6):**
      - **Magnesium ribbon** cleaned with sandpaper and ignited burns with a **dazzling white flame** to form a white powder of **magnesium oxide**.
      - Dissolving magnesium oxide in warm water turns **red litmus paper blue** $\to$ **Oxides of metals are generally BASIC in nature**.
      - **Sodium ($\text{Na}$) in Kerosene:** Sodium metal reacts **vigorously with oxygen and water**, generating a lot of heat (and catching fire); hence **sodium is stored immersed in kerosene**.
    - **Reaction of Non-Metals with Oxygen & Water (Acidic Oxides — Activities 4.7 & 4.8):**
      - Burning powdered **sulfur** in a **deflagrating spoon** inside a gas jar produces **sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) gas**.
      - Dissolving sulfur dioxide gas in water forms **sulfurous acid**, which turns **blue litmus paper red** $\to$ **Oxides of non-metals are generally ACIDIC in nature**.
      - **Reaction with Water:** Non-metals (like sulfur) generally **do NOT react with water**. Because **phosphorus** catches fire spontaneously when exposed to atmospheric air, **phosphorus is stored in water**!
    - **Elements (*Dive Deeper*):** Metals and non-metals are subcategories of **elements** (substances that cannot be broken down into simpler substances; **118 elements** are known). Everyday materials like plastic, glass, wood, rubber, and paper are **not** elements.
  - **4.5 Everyday Uses of Non-Metals & Special Metals:**
    - **Oxygen:** Essential non-metal for respiration of all living beings and combustion.
    - **Carbon:** Fundamental building block of all life forms (constituent of proteins, fats, and carbohydrates).
    - **Nitrogen:** Used in manufacturing **fertilisers** and chemicals; essential nutrient for plant growth.
    - **Chlorine:** Non-metal widely used in **water purification**.
    - **Iodine:** Non-metal whose solution is applied on wounds as an **antiseptic**.
    - **Special Metals (*Science and Society*):** **Zirconium** (used in **atomic energy**), **Titanium** (used in **aerospace**), recycling of iron and aluminium, and traditional Indian metal crafts (**Dhokra, Bidriware, Pembarthi, Kamrupi**).
- **Key Terms & Chemical Clues:**
  - *Metallic lustre*, *malleability*, *ductility* ($1\text{ g}$ gold $\to 2\text{ km}$ wire), *brittle*, *sonority / sonorous*, *thermal and electrical conductivity*, *sodium & potassium (soft metals, sodium stored in kerosene)*, *mercury (liquid metal)*, *rusting (requires both air and water)*, *silica gel*, *corrosion (brown on iron, green on copper, black on silver)*, *galvanisation (zinc coating)*, *Iron Pillar of Delhi (Chandragupta II)*, *burning magnesium ribbon (dazzling white flame, basic magnesium oxide)*, *deflagrating spoon*, *burning sulfur (sulfur dioxide $\to$ sulfurous acid, acidic)*, *phosphorus (stored in water)*, *chlorine (water purification)*, *iodine (antiseptic)*, *zirconium (atomic energy)*, *titanium (aerospace)*.
- **Mapping & Disambiguation Rules:**
  - **Map here** for physical properties of metals/non-metals (malleability, ductility, sonority, conductivity, soft metals $\text{Na}/\text{K}$, liquid $\text{Hg}$), the 3-bottle iron rusting experiment, corrosion colours, basic nature of $\text{MgO}$ vs. acidic nature of $\text{SO}_2$, storage of sodium in kerosene vs. phosphorus in water, and uses of non-metals ($\text{O}_2, \text{C}, \text{N}_2, \text{Cl}_2, \text{I}_2$).
  - **Disambiguation with Class 8 Chapter 8 (`sci-8-08-elements-compounds-mixtures`):** If a question asks about **metalloids (Silicon, Boron)**, **Bromine as a liquid non-metal**, **alloys composition (brass, bronze/*Kamsya*, stainless steel)**, **electrolysis of water**, or **Iron + Sulfur mixture vs. Iron Sulfide ($\text{FeS}$) compound**, map to `sci-8-08-elements-compounds-mixtures`.

### `sci-7-05-changes-physical-chemical` — Chapter 5: Changes Around Us: Physical and Chemical
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 1 | Chapter 5 | Book pp. 57–72 (PDF pp. 73–88) | **Sub-Discipline:** Chemistry (Physical & Chemical Reactions, Combustion, & Weathering)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Physical and Chemical Changes, Reversible and Irreversible Changes, Lime Water Test for Carbon Dioxide, Vinegar and Baking Soda Reaction, Combustion and Ignition Temperature (Fire Triangle), Burning of a Candle, Weathering and Erosion of Rocks
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **5.1 & 5.2 Physical Changes (Activities 5.1 & 5.2):**
    - **Definition:** Changes in which only **physical properties** (such as shape, size, colour, or physical state — solid, liquid, gas) change and **no new substance is formed**.
    - **Examples:** Melting of ice, boiling/evaporating water, condensing steam, chopping vegetables, cutting or tearing paper, folding paper (origami), inflating/deflating or pricking a balloon, crushing a piece of chalk into powder, rolling dough into *chapatis*, drying wet clothes, dissolving sugar/salt in water.
  - **5.3 Chemical Changes & Chemical Reactions:**
    - **Definition:** Changes in which **one or more new substances** with different properties are formed through a **chemical reaction** (represented by a **chemical equation**).
    - **Key Textbook Chemical Reactions:**
      1. **Lime Water Test for Carbon Dioxide (Activity 5.3):** Blowing exhaled air into freshly prepared **lime water (calcium hydroxide solution)** turns it **milky (cloudy)** due to the formation of an insoluble white precipitate of **calcium carbonate**:
         $$\text{Calcium hydroxide (Lime water)} + \text{Carbon dioxide} \to \text{Calcium carbonate (Insoluble white substance)} + \text{Water}$$
      2. **Vinegar (or Lemon Juice) + Baking Soda Reaction (Activity 5.4):** Mixing vinegar (acetic acid) or lemon juice (citric acid) with **baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate)** produces a fizzing, bubbling evolution of **carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) gas** (which turns lime water milky when passed through a delivery tube/straw):
         $$\text{Vinegar (or Lemon juice)} + \text{Baking soda} \to \text{Carbon dioxide} + \text{Other substances}$$
         *(Note: Vinegar + common salt or lemon juice + vinegar does NOT produce $\text{CO}_2$ or turn lime water milky).*
      3. **5.3.1 Rusting of Iron:** Iron reacts with moist air to form a new brown substance (**iron oxide / rust**) $\to$ chemical change.
      4. **5.3.2 Combustion & The Fire Triangle (Activities 5.5 & 5.6):**
         - **Combustion:** A chemical reaction in which a substance (**combustible substance / fuel** — wood, paper, cotton, kerosene, wax, magnesium) reacts with **oxygen** and produces **heat and/or light**:
           $$\text{Magnesium (Ribbon)} + \text{Oxygen (Air)} \to \text{Magnesium oxide (White powder)} + \text{Heat} + \text{Light}$$
         - **Role of Oxygen (Activity 5.5):** A burning candle covered with an inverted glass tumbler extinguishes once the oxygen inside is consumed (producing $\text{CO}_2$ and water vapour). **Fire Blanket Safety:** Wrapping a thick woollen/cotton blanket around a person whose clothes catch fire cuts off the air/oxygen supply; **never use a synthetic blanket/cloth**, as it melts and sticks to the skin!
         - **Ignition Temperature (Activity 5.6):** Focusing sunrays on paper with a magnifying glass heats the paper until it reaches the **minimum (lowest) temperature at which a substance catches fire**, called its **ignition temperature**.
         - **Fire Triangle (Three Requirements for Combustion — Fig. 5.8):**
           1. **Fuel** (combustible substance)
           2. **Oxygen** (supporter of combustion)
           3. **Heat** (to raise the fuel to its **ignition temperature**).
         - **Bioluminescence in Fireflies (*Fascinating Facts*):** Fireflies emit light in late evenings via an internal **chemical change that produces light WITHOUT heat**, known as **bioluminescence**.
  - **5.4 Can Physical and Chemical Changes Occur in the Same Process? (Activity 5.7):**
    - **Burning of a Candle (Michael Faraday's *Chemical History of a Candle*):**
      - **Physical Changes:** Melting of solid wax into liquid wax, drawing of liquid wax up the wick, evaporation (vapourisation) of liquid wax due to heat, and re-solidification of dripping wax.
      - **Chemical Change:** **Burning (combustion) of wax vapour** in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water vapour, heat, and light.
  - **5.5 Reversible vs. Irreversible Changes (Activity 5.8 & Table 5.2):**
    - **Reversible Changes:** Original object/substance and form can be recovered — melting ice, boiling and condensing water, dissolving sugar in water, folding a handkerchief, twisting a string, rolling up a mat.
    - **Irreversible Changes:** Original state cannot be brought back — **includes all chemical changes** (making popcorn from corn, making *idlis* from batter, ripening of fruits, curdling of milk, burning wood, composting leaves, rusting) **AND several physical changes** (chopping vegetables, tearing/cutting paper, pricking an inflated balloon, grinding wheat grains into flour).
  - **5.6 Desirable vs. Undesirable Changes:**
    - **Desirable:** Setting of milk into curd, ripening of fruits, cooking food, decomposing organic waste into **compost**.
    - **Undesirable:** Rusting of iron, decay of stored food, atmospheric pollution from fossil fuel combustion ($\text{CO}_2$) and solvent evaporation from drying paint.
  - **5.7 Some Slow Natural Changes (Weathering & Erosion):**
    - **5.7.1 Weathering of Rocks (Physical + Chemical Change):**
      - **Physical Weathering:** Temperature changes, growing tree roots, and freezing of water in cracks break large rocks into smaller **sediments** at the base of cliffs/mountains.
      - **Chemical Weathering:** Water and dissolved chemicals/air react with rock minerals — e.g., black **basalt** rock (which contains iron) reacts with water/air over long periods to form a **red-coloured layer of iron oxide**.
      - Together, these physical and chemical changes are called **weathering**, which leads to the **formation of soil**.
    - **5.7.2 Erosion (Physical Change):**
      - Movement and breakdown of rock pebbles, soil, and sediments from one location to another by flowing water and wind (e.g., landslides, smoothing of river pebbles); sediments settle at the bottom of lakes/oceans and harden over thousands of years into new rocks.

### `sci-7-07-heat-transfer-water-cycle` — Chapter 7: Heat Transfer in Nature
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 2 | Chapter 7 | Book pp. 1–16 (PDF pp. 5–20) | **Sub-Discipline:** Physics & Earth Science (Thermal Physics & Hydrology)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Heat and Modes of Heat Transfer (Conduction, Convection, Radiation), Good and Poor Conductors of Heat (Insulators), Sea Breeze and Land Breeze, Water Cycle, Infiltration, Groundwater and Aquifers
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **7.1 Conduction of Heat (Activity 7.1):**
    - **Definition:** The process of heat transfer from the **hotter part to the colder part** of an object in which heated particles pass heat to adjacent particles **without the particles themselves moving from their positions**.
    - **Primary Mode in Solids:** In solids, heat transfer takes place mainly by **conduction** (demonstrated by heating one end of a metal strip having 4 wax-attached pins I, II, III, IV — the pins fall sequentially from the heated end towards the colder end).
    - **Good Conductors vs. Poor Conductors (Insulators) of Heat:**
      - **Good Conductors of Heat:** Materials that allow heat to pass through them easily — **metals** (steel, copper, aluminium, iron), used for cooking utensils.
      - **Poor Conductors (Insulators) of Heat:** Materials that do not allow heat to pass through easily — **wood, plastic** (used for handles of saucepans/kettles), **glass, clay, porcelain** (used for tea/coffee cups to keep beverages hot longer), **wool, mud, cow dung, and air**.
      - **Applications of Trapped Air as an Insulator:**
        1. **Woollen Clothes & Two Thin Blankets:** Woollen fibres trap air; using **two thin blankets joined together** is warmer than one thick blanket because the **layer of air trapped between them** acts as a heat insulator.
        2. **Hollow Bricks & Himalayan Houses:** Outer walls built with **hollow bricks** trap air to keep houses warm in winter and cool in summer. In the **Mori block of Uttarkashi (Uttarakhand)**, traditional houses have double wooden walls filled with **cow dung and mud** (insulators) to prevent heat loss.
        3. **Double Tumbler:** Placing a tumbler of cold *lassi* inside a second tumbler traps an insulating air layer between them, keeping it cold longer.
  - **7.2 Convection (Activities 7.2–7.4):**
    - **Definition:** The process of heat transfer in **liquids and gases (fluids)** that takes place by the **actual movement of heated particles** from one place to another.
    - **Convection in Gases (Air — Activity 7.2):**
      - When air is heated (e.g., above a candle flame or fire), it **expands, occupies more space, becomes lighter (less dense), and rises up** (lifting an inverted paper cup balanced on a beam, expanding a balloon in the Sun, and carrying **smoke** upward — which is why **smoke detectors are always fixed on the ceiling** and a paper spiral suspended above a candle spins continuously).
    - **Convection in Liquids (Water — Activity 7.3):**
      - Placing a grain of **potassium permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4$)** at the bottom centre of a beaker of water heated by a candle produces a rising **coloured streak** as heated water becomes lighter and rises, while cooler, heavier water from the sides moves down to replace it, heating the entire volume of water. (Therefore, a thermometer placed at the **top** of a heated test tube of water records a much higher temperature than one near the sides/bottom if heated near the top).
    - **7.2.1 Differential Heating of Land & Water — Sea Breeze and Land Breeze (Activity 7.4):**
      - **Experimental Rule:** When identical bowls of **soil (land)** and **water** are placed in sunlight and then cooled indoors, **soil heats up faster than water during the day AND cools down faster than water at night**.
      - **Sea Breeze (Daytime — Fig. 7.7a):** Land heats up faster than the sea $\to$ warm air above the land rises $\to$ cooler air from the sea moves towards the land to take its place (**Sea Breeze**). Houses in coastal areas have windows facing the sea to receive the cooling sea breeze.
      - **Land Breeze (Night-time — Fig. 7.7b):** Land cools down faster than the sea, so the sea water remains warmer $\to$ warm air above the sea rises $\to$ cooler air from the land moves towards the sea (**Land Breeze**).
  - **7.3 Radiation:**
    - **Definition:** The mode of heat transfer directly from a hot body (like the **Sun**, a **fireplace**, or a cooling utensil) to surroundings **that does NOT require any material medium**. All objects continuously radiate and exchange heat with their surroundings.
    - **Summer vs. Winter Clothing Colours (*Dive Deeper*):**
      - **White / Light-coloured clothes** are preferred in **summers** because they **reflect most of the heat** falling on them.
      - **Dark-coloured / black clothes** are preferred in **winters** because dark surfaces **absorb more heat**.
    - **All Three Modes Acting Together (Fig. 7.8 & *Bukhari*):**
      - Heating water in a metal pan over a fire involves **conduction** (through the metal pan base), **convection** (circulation of water inside), and **radiation** (heat felt around the flame and pan).
      - The Himalayan ***Bukhari*** (an iron room-heating stove burning wood/charcoal with a chimney pipe and flat cooking top) also utilises all three heat transfer modes.
  - **7.4 Water Cycle, Infiltration, Groundwater, and Aquifers (Activity 7.5):**
    - **Water Cycle (Fig. 7.9):** Driven by the Sun's heat radiation — surface water undergoes **evaporation** and plants release water vapour via **transpiration**; rising water vapour cools and undergoes **condensation** to form clouds, returning to Earth as **precipitation** (rain, snow, hail), redistributing and conserving Earth's total water. (Also referenced in Varahamihira's ancient Sanskrit encyclopaedia ***Brihatsamhita***).
    - **7.4.1 Seepage of Water Beneath the Earth (Activity 7.5 & Table 7.5):**
      - **Infiltration:** The process of surface rainwater seeping down through soil and rocks into the ground.
      - **Seepage Rate Comparison:**
        $$\text{Gravel (Fastest seepage — wide, open, interconnected pore spaces)} > \text{Sand (Moderate/slow)} > \text{Clay (Very slow — tiny spaces)}$$
      - **Groundwater:** Water that seeps down and gets stored in the pore spaces of underground sediments and rock openings.
      - **Aquifers:** The underground layers of sediments and rocks that store water in their pore spaces (from which water is extracted via wells and borewells).
      - **Depletion & Replenishment:** Excessive extraction, loss of vegetation cover, and concrete urban surfaces reduce infiltration and deplete groundwater; mitigated via **rainwater harvesting**, **recharge pits**, and **Ice Stupas in Ladakh** (where winter mountain stream water is piped underground and sprayed into freezing air to build a tall cone-shaped artificial ice glacier that melts slowly in spring/summer for irrigation).

  - *Conduction (solids, no particle movement)*, *good conductors vs. poor conductors (insulators)*, *trapped air insulator (woollen clothes, two thin blankets, hollow bricks, Mori block Uttarkashi houses)*, *convection (liquids and gases, actual movement of particles)*, *potassium permanganate convection streak*, *sea breeze (day, sea $\to$ land)*, *land breeze (night, land $\to$ sea)*, *radiation (no medium required)*, *light vs. dark clothes*, *Bukhari*, *water cycle (evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation)*, *Brihatsamhita*, *infiltration*, *seepage (gravel $>$ sand $>$ clay)*, *groundwater*, *aquifers*, *rainwater harvesting / recharge pits*, *Ice Stupa (Ladakh)*.

### `sci-7-08-time-and-motion` — Chapter 8: Measurement of Time and Motion
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 2 | Chapter 8 | Book pp. 17–32 (PDF pp. 21–36) | **Sub-Discipline:** Physics (Kinematics & Timekeeping)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Motion and Time, Ancient Clocks (Sundial, Samrat Yantra, Ghatika-yantra), Simple Pendulum and Time Period, SI Unit of Time, Speed and Average Speed Calculations, Speedometer and Odometer, Uniform and Non-Uniform Linear Motion
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **8.1 Measurement of Time:**
    - **Historical Timekeeping Devices:**
      1. **Sundials:** Measure time from the changing position/length of a shadow cast in sunlight.
         - **Samrat Yantra at Jantar Mantar, Jaipur (UNESCO World Heritage Site):** The **world's largest stone sundial** ($27\text{ m}$ high); its shadow moves at $\approx 1\text{ mm per second}$ along a finely marked scale, measuring time intervals as short as **$2\text{ seconds}$** (measures local solar time).
      2. **Water Clocks:**
         - **Outflow type:** Water drips out of a vessel with time markings.
         - **Floating / Sinking Bowl type (*Ghatika-yantra*):** Mentioned in Kautilya's ***Arthasastra***, ***Sardulakarnavadana***, and by **Aryabhata**. A hemispherical copper bowl with a fine hole at the bottom floated on water and took **exactly $24\text{ minutes}$** to fill and sink (announced by drums, conch shells, or gongs). The unit measured was **$1\text{ ghatika (ghati)} = 24\text{ minutes}$**, dividing a $24\text{-hour}$ day into **$60\text{ equal ghatis}$**.
      3. **Hourglass (Sand clock)** and **Candle clocks**.
    - **8.1.1 The Simple Pendulum (Activity 8.2):**
      - **Historical Discovery (*Know a Scientist*):** **Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642) observed a swinging ceiling lamp in a church using his own **pulse beat** to time it, discovering that a pendulum of a given length always takes the **same time to complete each swing (oscillation)**. Inspired by Galileo, **Christiaan Huygens** (1629–1695) invented the **pendulum clock** in **1656** (patented in **1657**).
      - **Structure & Motion:** Consists of a small metallic ball or stone (**bob**) suspended from a rigid support by a long thread. When displaced slightly from its rest (**mean position $O$**) and released, it executes **oscillatory motion**, which is **periodic** in nature.
      - **One Complete Oscillation:** Movement of the bob from mean position $O \to \text{extreme position } A \to \text{extreme position } B \to \text{back to } O$ (or from extreme $A \to B \to A$).
      - **Time Period ($T$):** The time taken by a pendulum to complete **one oscillation**:
        $$\text{Time Period } (T) = \frac{\text{Total time taken for } N \text{ oscillations}}{N}$$
      - **Laws of the Simple Pendulum:** At a given location, the time period of a simple pendulum **depends ONLY on its length** (a longer pendulum has a longer time period) and is **independent of the mass of the bob** (or small changes in displacement).
      - **Modern Clocks:** Quartz clocks, digital watches, Braille/talking watches, and **atomic clocks** (which lose only **$1\text{ second}$ in millions of years**!). High-precision intervals include the **millisecond** ($10^{-3}\text{ s}$, used in Olympic sprints and **ECG** heart monitors) and **microsecond** ($10^{-6}\text{ s}$, used in smartphones and computers).
    - **8.1.2 SI Unit of Time & Writing Conventions:**
      - **SI Unit:** **second** (symbol: **`s`**). Larger units: **minute** (**`min`**, $1\text{ min} = 60\text{ s}$) and **hour** (**`h`**, $1\text{ h} = 60\text{ min} = 3600\text{ s}$).
      - **Mandatory SI Rules (*Dive Deeper*):** Unit names and symbols (`s`, `min`, `h`) are always written in **lowercase singular** without a trailing period, with a space between the number and symbol. Writing `sec`, `secs`, or `hrs` is **incorrect**.
  - **8.2 & 8.3 Slow or Fast & Speed (Activities 8.3 & 8.4):**
    - **Definition & Formulas:** Speed is the distance covered by an object in a unit time (in this textbook, "speed" refers to **average speed** over the journey):
      $$\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Total distance covered}}{\text{Total time taken}}$$
      $$\text{Total distance covered} = \text{Speed} \times \text{Total time taken}, \qquad \text{Total time taken} = \frac{\text{Total distance covered}}{\text{Speed}}$$
    - **Units & Conversion:**
      - **SI Unit:** **metre per second ($\text{m/s}$)**.
      - **Practical / Vehicle Unit:** **kilometre per hour ($\text{km/h}$)**.
      - **Conversion Factor:**
        $$1\text{ m/s} = \frac{3600\text{ m}}{1000\text{ m/km}}/\text{h} = 3.6\text{ km/h} \iff 1\text{ km/h} = \frac{1000\text{ m}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s}$$
    - **Dashboard Instruments:**
      - **Speedometer:** Measures and displays the vehicle's instantaneous **speed in $\text{km/h}$**.
      - **Odometer:** Measures the total **distance travelled** by the vehicle in **kilometres ($\text{km}$)**.
  - **8.4 Uniform and Non-uniform Linear Motion (Table 8.3):**
    - **Linear Motion:** Motion of an object along a **straight line**.
    - **Uniform Linear Motion:** An object moving along a straight line at a **constant (unchanging) speed** — it covers **equal distances in equal intervals of time**.
    - **Non-uniform Linear Motion:** An object moving along a straight line whose **speed keeps changing** — it covers **unequal distances in equal intervals of time** (most real-world motions, like a train starting from one station and stopping at the next, or a car in city traffic).

  - *Sundial*, *Samrat Yantra (Jantar Mantar, Jaipur)*, *water clock*, *Ghatika-yantra ($1\text{ ghati} = 24\text{ min}$, $60\text{ ghatis} = 24\text{ h}$, Kautilya's Arthasastra, Aryabhata)*, *hourglass*, *candle clock*, *Galileo Galilei (pendulum isochronism)*, *Christiaan Huygens (pendulum clock, 1656)*, *simple pendulum*, *bob*, *mean position*, *extreme position*, *oscillatory & periodic motion*, *time period ($T = t/N$, depends on length, independent of bob mass)*, *atomic clock*, *millisecond*, *microsecond*, $\text{Speed} = \text{Distance}/\text{Time}$, $\text{m/s}$ vs. $\text{km/h}$, *speedometer*, *odometer*, *uniform vs. non-uniform linear motion*.

### `sci-7-09-life-processes-animals` — Chapter 9: Life Processes in Animals
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 2 | Chapter 9 | Book pp. 33–48 (PDF pp. 37–52) | **Sub-Discipline:** Biology (Human & Animal Physiology — Digestion, Respiration, & Circulation)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Nutrition in Animals, Human Digestive System (Alimentary Canal & Digestive Glands), Action of Saliva on Starch, Digestion in Ruminants (Cows) and Birds (Gizzard), Respiration in Organisms, Human Respiratory System and Breathing Mechanism, Breathing in Fish, Frogs, and Earthworms
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **9.1 Nutrition in Animals:**
    - **9.1.1 Digestion in Human Beings (The Alimentary Canal & Associated Glands — Figs. 9.1 & 9.4):**
      - **Pathway of Food:** $\text{Mouth (Buccal Cavity)} \to \text{Oesophagus (Food pipe)} \to \text{Stomach} \to \text{Small Intestine} \to \text{Large Intestine} \to \text{Rectum} \to \text{Anus}$.
      - **Associated Digestive Glands:** **Salivary glands**, **Liver**, and **Pancreas**.
      - **Step-by-Step Breakdown Across Compartments:**
        1. **Mouth Cavity:**
           - **Mechanical Digestion:** Teeth crush and chew food into small pieces; tongue mixes food with saliva.
           - **Chemical Digestion by Saliva (Activity 9.1):** Saliva contains a digestive juice that breaks down **starch (complex carbohydrate) into simple sugars** (why *chapati* or boiled rice tastes sweet when chewed thoroughly).
           - **Iodine Test on Boiled Rice (Test Tube A) vs. Chewed Boiled Rice (Test Tube B):** Adding 3–4 drops of **iodine solution** turns boiled rice in Test Tube A **blue-black** (confirming starch), whereas chewed boiled rice in Test Tube B shows **no colour change (or very faint)** because salivary digestion has converted the starch into sugar!
        2. **Food Pipe (Oesophagus — Fig. 9.2):**
           - Muscular walls gently **contract and relax in a wave-like motion (peristalsis)** to push swallowed food down into the stomach.
        3. **Stomach (Fig. 9.3):**
           - Walls contract and relax to churn food into a semi-liquid mass and mix it with three secretions from the inner stomach lining:
             - **Digestive juice:** Breaks down **proteins** into simpler components.
             - **Acid ($\text{HCl}$):** Provides an acidic medium to help break down proteins and **kills many harmful bacteria** that enter with food.
             - **Mucus:** Protects the inner lining of the stomach from being damaged by its own acid.
           - **Historical Discovery (*Fascinating Facts*):** In **1822**, American doctor **William Beaumont** studied human stomach digestion directly through a permanent unhealed gunshot opening (fistula) in the stomach of **Alexis St. Martin**.
        4. **Small Intestine ($\approx 6\text{ metres}$ long — the longest part of the alimentary canal!):**
           - **Complete Digestion via Three Secretions:**
             - **Liver:** Secretes **bile**, which is **mildly basic** in nature — it **neutralises the acid** coming from the stomach and **breaks down fats into tiny droplets** (making fat digestion easier).
             - **Pancreas:** Secretes **pancreatic juice**, which is also **basic** (neutralises acid) and breaks down **carbohydrates, proteins, and fats**.
             - **Inner Lining of Small Intestine:** Secretes intestinal digestive juice that completes the breakdown of **fats, proteins, and partially digested carbohydrates** into simplest absorbable forms.
           - **Absorption of Nutrients (Fig. 9.5):** The thin inner wall of the small intestine has thousands of microscopic **finger-like projections (villi)** that vastly **increase the surface area** for efficient absorption of digested nutrients into blood vessels.
           - **Celiac Disease (*Science and Society*):** An autoimmune/digestive condition in which the body reacts adversely to **gluten** (a protein in **wheat, barley, and rye**), damaging the inner lining of the small intestine and impairing nutrient absorption; managed by consuming naturally gluten-free **millets (*jowar, bajra, ragi*)**.
        5. **Large Intestine ($\approx 1.5\text{ metres}$ long, wider than small intestine), Rectum, & Anus:**
           - Absorbs **most of the remaining water and some salts** from undigested food, forming semi-solid waste (**stool**), which is stored in the **rectum** and expelled through the **anus** (**egestion**).
           - Houses beneficial **gut bacteria** that ferment fibre and produce essential nutrients; supported by fibre-rich foods, traditional **fermented foods** (*curd, buttermilk, shrikhand, kanji, pickles, gundruk, poita bhat*), and digestive spices (*Charaka Samhita*: ginger, black pepper, cumin).
    - **9.1.2 Digestion in Other Animals (Ruminants and Birds — Figs. 9.6 & 9.7):**
      - **Ruminants (Grass-eating mammals like cows, buffaloes, goats):** Quickly swallow partially chewed grass into a specialised stomach chamber called the **rumen**, where partial digestion occurs; later, the partially digested food (**cud**) is returned to the mouth in small lumps for thorough chewing (~8 hours a day) — a process called **rumination**.
      - **Birds:** Do **not** have teeth; instead, they have a muscular stomach chamber called a **gizzard**, which grinds and crushes food by muscular contraction and relaxation with the help of swallowed **grit (small stones)**.
  - **9.2 Respiration in Animals:**
    - **9.2.1 Human Respiratory System & Breathing Mechanism (Figs. 9.8–9.13):**
      - **Respiratory Pathway:** $\text{Nostrils} \to \text{Nasal passages (tiny hair \& mucus trap dust/dirt — why we should breathe through the nose, not the mouth)} \to \text{Windpipe (Trachea)} \to \text{Two branches (Bronchi)} \to \text{Left \& Right Lungs (protected by the Rib cage)} \to \text{Alveoli (tiny balloon-like sacs)}$.
      - **Mechanism of Breathing (Activity 9.2 Bottle-Balloon-Rubber Sheet Model: Balloons = Lungs, Rubber sheet = Diaphragm):**

        | Phase | Movement of Ribs | Movement of Diaphragm (Dome-shaped muscle) | Chest Cavity Volume | Air Flow |
        | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
        | **Inhalation (Breathing In)** | Move **up and outwards** | Moves **downwards** (pulls rubber sheet down) | **Increases** | Air is **drawn into** the lungs (balloons inflate) |
        | **Exhalation (Breathing Out)** | Move **down and inwards** | Moves **upwards** (relaxes) | **Decreases** | Air is **pushed out** of the lungs (balloons deflate) |

      - **Inhaled vs. Exhaled Air Composition (Fig. 9.13 & Activity 9.3 Lime Water Test):**
        - **Inhaled Air:** **$\approx 21\%$ Oxygen**, **$\approx 0.04\%$ Carbon dioxide**.
        - **Exhaled Air:** **$\approx 16\text{–}17\%$ Oxygen**, **$\approx 4\text{–}5\%$ Carbon dioxide** (turns freshly prepared **lime water milky** when exhaled through a straw, whereas normal air pumped with a syringe/*pichkari* does not).
      - **Gas Exchange in Alveoli, Circulatory System, & Cellular Respiration:**
        - Across the thin walls of the **alveoli**, oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled.
        - **Circulatory System:** Consists of the **heart, blood, and blood vessels**; the heart pumps blood to transport oxygen and absorbed nutrients to every cell and carry away waste products.
        - **Breathing vs. Respiration:** **Breathing** is a **physical process** of gas exchange (inhalation/exhalation), whereas **respiration** is a **chemical process** inside body cells where oxygen breaks down glucose to release usable energy:
          $$\text{Glucose} + \text{Oxygen} \to \text{Carbon dioxide} + \text{Water} + \text{Energy}$$
    - **9.2.2 Breathing Organs Across Animals (Fig. 9.14):**
      - **Lungs:** Humans, elephants, lions, cows, goats, **birds**, **lizards**, **snakes**.
      - **Gills:** Aquatic animals like **fish** and **tadpoles** (gills are richly supplied with blood vessels to exchange dissolved $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2$ directly with water).
      - **Dual Breathing in Amphibians (Frogs):** **Tadpoles** breathe through **gills**; **adult frogs** breathe through **lungs on land** and through their **moist skin when in water**.
      - **Moist Skin:** **Earthworms** breathe exclusively through their moist skin.

### `sci-7-10-life-processes-plants` — Chapter 10: Life Processes in Plants
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 2 | Chapter 10 | Book pp. 49–64 (PDF pp. 53–68) | **Sub-Discipline:** Biology (Botany & Plant Physiology)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Nutrition in Plants, Photosynthesis (Raw Materials, Products, and Word Equation), Starch Test in Leaves (Alcohol Decolourisation & Iodine Test), Moll's Half-Leaf Experiment (Caustic Soda), Stomata, Transport in Plants (Xylem and Phloem), Respiration in Plants
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **10.1 & 10.2 How Do Plants Produce Food? — Photosynthesis:**
    - **10.2.1 Leaves as 'Food Factories' & The Starch Test (Activities 10.1–10.3):**
      - Leaves are generally **broad and flat** to capture maximum sunlight and contain the green pigment **chlorophyll**.
      - Food is synthesised as **glucose** (a simple carbohydrate used for immediate energy) and converted into **starch** (a complex carbohydrate) for storage in leaves and other plant parts (like potatoes).
      - **Standard Leaf Starch Test Procedure (Activity 10.2 — Fig. 10.2):**
        1. Boil the leaf in water for 5 minutes to soften its tissues/cell walls.
        2. Dip the leaf in a test tube containing **alcohol** and heat the test tube in a **boiling water bath** (never place alcohol directly over a flame) until the leaf is **decolourised** (chlorophyll is extracted so the subsequent colour change is clearly visible).
        3. Rinse and add a few drops of **dilute iodine solution** $\to$ appearance of a **blue-black colour** confirms the presence of **starch**.
      - **Variegated Leaf & Darkness Experiment (Activity 10.3):** A leaf with green and non-green patches turns **blue-black ONLY on the green patches when kept in sunlight**; a plant kept in the **dark for 36 hours** shows **no blue-black colour** anywhere, proving that **both chlorophyll and sunlight are essential for photosynthesis**.
      - **Red, Violet, and Brown Leaves (*Fascinating Facts*):** Leaves with red, violet, or brown colouration **DO contain chlorophyll** and **DO perform photosynthesis** (confirmed by the iodine test); the green colour is merely masked by higher quantities of other coloured pigments.
    - **10.2.2 Role of Carbon Dioxide — Half-Leaf Experiment with Caustic Soda (Activity 10.4 — Fig. 10.3):**
      - A potted plant is **destarched** by keeping it in the dark for 2–3 days.
      - Half of one leaf is inserted through a split cork into a wide-mouthed bottle containing **caustic soda (sodium hydroxide, $\text{NaOH}$) solution**, which **absorbs carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$)** from the air inside the bottle, while the other half remains exposed to outside air in sunlight.
      - On testing with iodine, the **outer half turns blue-black**, whereas the **inner half (inside the $\text{NaOH}$ bottle) does NOT turn blue-black**, proving that **carbon dioxide is essential for photosynthesis**.
    - **10.2.3 Release of Oxygen During Photosynthesis (Activity 10.5 — Fig. 10.4):**
      - An aquatic plant placed under an inverted funnel and water-filled test tube in **sunlight** releases bubbles of gas that collect at the top of the test tube (no bubbles form in the dark set-up).
      - Inserting a **lit matchstick** into the collected gas causes it to burn with an **intense bright flame**, proving that **oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) gas is released during photosynthesis**.
      - **Complete Word Equation for Photosynthesis:**
        $$\text{Carbon dioxide} + \text{Water} \xrightarrow[\text{Chlorophyll}]{\text{Sunlight}} \text{Glucose (Carbohydrate)} + \text{Oxygen}$$
    - **10.2.4 Gas Exchange Through Stomata (Activity 10.6 — Fig. 10.6):**
      - Microscopic examination of a lower leaf peel (e.g., *Rhoeo*, money plant, hibiscus) shows tiny pores on the leaf surface called **stomata**, which regulate the exchange of **carbon dioxide and oxygen** during photosynthesis and respiration.
  - **10.3 Transport in Plants (Activity 10.7 — Figs. 10.7 & 10.8):**
    - **10.3.1 Transport of Water and Minerals — Xylem:**
      - Placing a white-flowered plant twig (*sadabahar* / balsam) in **red-coloured water** stains the stem vascular bundles, leaf veins, and flower petals red, demonstrating that **water and dissolved soil minerals** absorbed by roots are transported upward to all parts of the plant through thin tube-like structures called **xylem**.
    - **10.3.2 Transport of Food — Phloem:**
      - Food (glucose/sucrose) synthesised in the leaves is transported to all parts of the plant (including roots, stems, fruits, seeds, and underground storage tubers like **potato**) through another set of thin tube-like structures called **phloem**.
  - **10.4 Respiration in Plants (Activity 10.8 — Fig. 10.9):**
    - **Germinating Seeds Experiment:** Soaked **germinating *moong* bean seeds** kept in a dark conical flask for 24 hours release **carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$)** gas, which turns **lime water milky** in a connected test tube.
    - **Plant Respiration Equation & Universality:** All parts of a plant (**both green and non-green**, including roots and seeds) carry out **respiration** continuously to break down glucose using oxygen and release energy for growth:
      $$\text{Glucose} + \text{Oxygen} \to \text{Carbon dioxide} + \text{Water} + \text{Energy}$$
    - **Photosynthesis vs. Respiration Balance:** In a sealed **bottle garden** (Fig. 10.13) or a **water plant + snail** test tube in sunlight (Fig. 10.12), $\text{CO}_2$ released during respiration is used by the plant for photosynthesis, and $\text{O}_2$ released during photosynthesis is used for respiration!

### `sci-7-11-light-shadows-reflections` — Chapter 11: Light: Shadows and Reflections
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 2 | Chapter 11 | Book pp. 65–80 (PDF pp. 69–84) | **Sub-Discipline:** Physics (Geometrical Optics)
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Light Shadows and Reflections, Luminous and Non-Luminous Objects, Rectilinear Propagation of Light, Transparent Translucent and Opaque Materials, Shadow Characteristics, Reflection by a Plane Mirror and Lateral Inversion, Pinhole Camera, Periscope and Kaleidoscope
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **11.1 Sources of Light — Luminous vs. Non-Luminous Objects:**
    - **Luminous Objects:** Objects that **emit their own light**:
      - **Natural:** The **Sun** (primary natural source of light and heat on Earth; sunlight takes **$\approx 8\text{ minutes and } 20\text{ seconds}$** to reach the Earth), **stars** (e.g., **Pole Star**), lightning, and bioluminescent organisms like **fireflies**.
      - **Artificial:** Fire, candle, oil lamps, and electric lamps (incandescent bulbs, tube lights, CFLs, and **LED lamps** — which consume much less power, last longer, and are brighter).
    - **Non-Luminous Objects:** Objects that **do NOT emit their own light** and are seen only when they reflect light from a luminous source — e.g., the **Moon**, planets (**Mars, Venus**), **mirrors**, trees, and furniture.
  - **11.2 Rectilinear Propagation of Light (Activities 11.1 & 11.2):**
    - **Principle:** **Light travels in a straight line.**
    - **Experimental Proofs:**
      1. **Three-Matchbox Pinhole Alignment (Fig. 11.4):** Torchlight passes through pinholes in three matchboxes onto a screen **only when all three holes are aligned in a straight line**.
      2. **Straight vs. Bent Pipe (Fig. 11.5):** A candle flame is visible through a **straight hollow pipe**, but **cannot be seen when the pipe is bent**.
      3. **Laser Beam in Milky Water:** Follows a straight line path.
  - **11.3 Transparent, Translucent, and Opaque Materials (Activity 11.3 & Table 11.1):**
    - **Transparent Materials:** Allow light to pass through **almost completely** (clear glass, clean water, air).
    - **Translucent Materials:** Allow light to pass through **only partially** (tracing paper, butter paper, frosted glass).
    - **Opaque Materials:** Do **not** allow any light to pass through (cardboard, wood, metal, thick cloth, stone).
  - **11.4 Shadow Formation (Activity 11.4 & Table 11.2):**
    - **Definition & Three Essentials:** A **shadow** is the dark patch formed on a screen when an opaque object blocks the straight-line path of light. To form and observe a shadow, **three things are required**:
      1. A **source of light**
      2. An **opaque (or translucent) object**
      3. A **screen** (wall, floor, ground). *(A bird flying high in the sky does not cast a visible shadow on the ground because the object is too far from the ground screen).*
    - **Properties of Shadows:**
      - **Darkness:** **Opaque** objects form **dark shadows**; **translucent** objects form **lighter (fainter) shadows**; some transparent objects form very faint shadows.
      - **Size of Shadow vs. Distance (Fig. 11.18):**
        - Moving the **object closer to the light source** (or moving the torch closer to the object) makes the shadow **LARGER**.
        - Moving the **object closer to the screen** (or moving the torch farther away) makes the shadow **SMALLER**.
      - **Shape & Colour:** Tilting the object changes the shadow's shape; however, **changing the colour of the opaque object (or covering the torch with coloured transparent paper) does NOT change the dark colour of the shadow itself** (a red ball and a green ball both cast a dark black/grey shadow!).
      - **Traditional Indian Shadow Puppetry (*Our Cultural Heritage*):**
        - **Karnataka:** ***Togalu Gombeyaata***
        - **Andhra Pradesh:** ***Keelu Bomme*** and ***Tholu Bommalata***
        - **Maharashtra:** ***Charma Bahuli Natya***
        - **Odisha:** ***Ravana Chhaya***
        - **Kerala:** ***Tholpavakoothu***
        - **Tamil Nadu:** ***Bommalattam***
  - **11.5 & 11.6 Reflection of Light & Image in a Plane Mirror (Activities 11.5–11.8):**
    - **Reflection of Light:** The change in the direction of light when it falls on a shiny surface or mirror.
    - **Five Characteristics of the Image Formed by a Plane Mirror:**
      1. **Erect (upright).**
      2. **Of the exact same size** as the object.
      3. **Cannot be obtained on a screen** (virtual image).
      4. **Formed at the same distance behind the mirror** as the object is placed in front of the mirror.
      5. **Laterally Inverted (Left–Right Reversal):** The left side of the object appears as the right side of the image and vice versa (which is why **`AMBULANCE`** is written laterally inverted on ambulances so drivers ahead read it correctly in their rear-view mirrors, whereas symmetric capital letters like **`A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y`** appear identical in a plane mirror).
    - ***Aranmula Kannadi* (*Fascinating Facts*):** A centuries-old traditional handmade **metal-alloy mirror** (front-surface polished metal, not glass) crafted in **Aranmula, Kerala**.
  - **11.7 Pinhole Camera (Activities 11.9 & 11.10 — Figs. 11.12 & 11.13):**
    - **Principle:** Works on the **rectilinear (straight-line) propagation of light** passing through a tiny pinhole onto a translucent tracing-paper screen.
    - **Image Characteristics (vs. Shadow & Plane Mirror):** Forms a **real, inverted (upside-down) image** on the screen that **preserves the actual colours and details** of the bright object.
  - **11.8 Optical Devices Based on Plane Mirrors (Figs. 11.14 & 11.15):**
    - **11.8.1 Periscope:** Consists of **two plane mirrors** fixed parallel to each other (facing each other at $45^\circ$ angles) at the bends of a **Z-shaped tube**. Uses **successive reflection from two mirrors** to see over obstacles; used in **submarines, tanks, and soldiers' bunkers**.
    - **11.8.2 Kaleidoscope:** Consists of **three rectangular plane mirror strips** joined into a **triangular prism** inside a cylindrical tube containing coloured bangle pieces and a tracing-paper end. Produces infinite **symmetrical patterns due to multiple reflections** between the three inclined mirrors (used by artists and textile designers).

  - *Luminous vs. non-luminous objects*, *8 minutes 20 seconds (sunlight travel time to Earth)*, *rectilinear propagation of light*, *transparent, translucent, opaque*, *shadow*, *Togalu Gombeyaata (Karnataka)*, *Tholu Bommalata (AP)*, *Ravana Chhaya (Odisha)*, *Tholpavakoothu (Kerala)*, *Bommalattam (TN)*, *Charma Bahuli Natya (Maharashtra)*, *reflection of light*, *plane mirror image (erect, same size, same distance, cannot be obtained on screen, laterally inverted)*, *Aranmula Kannadi (Kerala metal mirror)*, *pinhole camera (inverted, real image on screen)*, *periscope (2 plane mirrors in Z-shaped tube)*, *kaleidoscope (3 plane mirrors in triangular prism, multiple reflections)*.

### `sci-7-12-earth-moon-sun` — Chapter 12: Earth, Moon, and the Sun
- **Book Metadata:** Class 7 Science Part 2 | Chapter 12 | Book pp. 81–98 (PDF pp. 85–102) | **Sub-Discipline:** Astronomy & Earth Science
- **Canonical Topic Aliases:** Rotation and Revolution of the Earth, Day and Night, Apparent Motion of Stars and Pole Star (Dhruva Tara), Foucault Pendulum and Aryabhatiya, Causes of Seasons (Axial Tilt), Solstices and Equinoxes, Solar Eclipse and Lunar Eclipse (Grahan)
- **Core Concepts & Subtopics:**
  - **12.1 Rotation of the Earth (Activities 12.1–12.3):**
    - **Axis & Direction of Rotation:** The Earth spins on its own imaginary **axis of rotation** (passing through the geographic **North Pole** and **South Pole**) from **West to East** (which is **anti-clockwise when viewed from above the North Pole**), completing one rotation in **about $24\text{ hours}$**.
    - **Effects of Earth's West-to-East Rotation:**
      1. **Cycle of Day and Night (Fig. 12.5):** The half of the spherical Earth facing the Sun experiences **daytime**, while the opposite half in darkness experiences **night-time**. Because Earth rotates from West to East, **eastern states of India (e.g., Assam, Jharkhand) experience sunrise earlier than western states (e.g., Gujarat)**, and when it is daytime in India it is night-time in the USA.
      2. **Apparent East-to-West Motion of Celestial Bodies:** Just as a rider on an anti-clockwise merry-go-round sees stationary trees move clockwise, observers on the West-to-East rotating Earth see the **Sun, Moon, and stars appear to rise in the East, move across the sky, and set in the West**.
      3. **Stationary Pole Star (*Dhruva Tara*) & Revolving Big Dipper (*Saptarishi*) (Fig. 12.7):** Because the Earth's axis of rotation points almost directly towards the **Pole Star** in the Northern Hemisphere, the **Pole Star appears stationary** in the sky while all other stars and constellations (like the **Big Dipper / *Saptarishi***) appear to revolve around it (creating circular **star trails** in long-exposure photographs).
    - **Foucault Pendulum (*Fascinating Facts*):** In the mid-19th century, French physicist **Léon Foucault** used a long heavy pendulum (**Foucault pendulum**) to provide the **first direct simple demonstration of the Earth's rotation**. A **$22\text{-metre}$ Foucault pendulum** hangs from the skylight in the **Constitution Hall of the new Parliament building in New Delhi**.
    - **Aryabhata's Boat Analogy & Rotation Period (*Our Scientific Heritage*):** In ***Aryabhatiya* (Golapada, Verse 9)**, ancient Indian astronomer **Aryabhata** explained the apparent westward motion of stationary stars using the analogy of a man in a boat moving forward seeing stationary objects on the shore move backward, and stated Earth's rotation period as **$23\text{ hours } 56\text{ minutes } 4.1\text{ seconds}$** (remarkably close to the modern sidereal day!).
  - **12.2 Revolution of the Earth & Seasons:**
    - **Orbit & Period:** While rotating on its axis, the Earth revolves around the Sun along a nearly circular **orbit**, taking **about $365\text{ days and } 6\text{ hours}$** ($\approx 1\text{ year}$) per revolution.
    - **12.2.1 Changing View of the Night Sky Over a Year (Fig. 12.8):**
      - Because the Earth faces different directions in space as it revolves around the Sun, **different stars and constellations appear in the night sky at sunset in different months** (a group of stars rising at midnight on 21 June will rise at midnight again one full year later on 21 June next year, and appears slightly earlier each night). (*Bhil and Pawara* indigenous communities of the Tapi Valley in western India used star patterns to predict the arrival of monsoon rains).
    - **12.2.2 Why Seasons Occur on the Earth (Figs. 12.9–12.11):**
      - **True Cause of Seasons:** Seasons occur due to the combination of:
        1. The **tilt of the Earth's axis of rotation** with respect to its orbital plane (maintained in a fixed direction throughout its orbit), AND
        2. The **spherical shape of the Earth**.
      - **Crucial Misconceptions Debunked (*Dive Deeper*):** Seasons are **NOT** caused by the tilted hemisphere being "closer" to the Sun, nor by the Earth's slightly oval orbit varying its distance from the Sun! In fact, the **Earth is closest to the Sun in January** ($\approx 147\text{ million km}$ vs. $\approx 152\text{ million km}$ in July).
      - **Opposition of Seasons Between Hemispheres:**

        | Month / Position | Northern Hemisphere (e.g., India) | Southern Hemisphere (e.g., Australia) | Polar Day / Night |
        | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
        | **June (~21 June: Summer Solstice in NH)** | **Tilted towards the Sun:** Sunrays fall more directly (spread over a **smaller area** $\to$ more intense heating) and daytime lasts **$> 12\text{ hours}$** $\to$ **SUMMER** (**Longest day** on **21 June**). | **Tilted away from the Sun:** Sunrays fall obliquely over a larger area and daytime lasts $< 12\text{ hours}$ $\to$ **WINTER**. | **North Pole** has **24 hours of sunlight**; **South Pole** has **24 hours of darkness** (6 months continuous sun at North Pole from 21 March to 22 Sept). |
        | **December (~22 Dec: Winter Solstice in NH)** | **Tilted away from the Sun:** Sunrays spread over a **larger area** (less intense) and daytime lasts **$< 12\text{ hours}$** $\to$ **WINTER** (**Shortest day / longest night** on **22 December**). | **Tilted towards the Sun:** More intense sunlight and longer daytime $\to$ **SUMMER** (why cricketers visiting Australia in December pack summer clothes!). | **North Pole** has **24 hours of darkness**; **South Pole** has **24 hours of sunlight**. |
        | **~21 March (Spring Equinox) & ~23 Sept (Autumn Equinox)** | Neither pole is tilted towards the Sun $\to$ **Equal day ($12\text{ h}$) and night ($12\text{ h}$)** across the globe. | Equal day ($12\text{ h}$) and night ($12\text{ h}$). | Transition day at both poles. *(At the **Equator**, day and night are always $12\text{ h}$ each all year round, and seasonal differences are minimal).* |

  - **12.3 Eclipses (*Grahan* — Activity 12.4 & Figs. 12.12–12.16):**
    - **Apparent Size Principle (Thumb Activity 12.4):** Although the Moon's physical diameter is much smaller than the Sun's, the Moon is **much closer to the Earth** than the Sun, making their **apparent sizes in the sky nearly equal**.
    - **Transit of Venus (*Dive Deeper*):** Planets **Mercury and Venus** are physically larger than the Moon, but because they are much farther from Earth, their apparent sizes are tiny dots that can **never block the Sun**. When Venus passes directly between the Sun and Earth, it appears as a tiny black dot moving across the solar disc, called a **Transit of Venus**.
    - **12.3.1 Solar Eclipse ($\text{Sun} \to \text{Moon} \to \text{Earth}$):**
      - Occurs when the **Moon comes directly between the Sun and the Earth**, obstructing sunlight and casting the **Moon's shadow on a small area of the Earth**:
        - **Total Solar Eclipse:** Moon completely covers the Sun's disc for observers in the small dark shadow zone (lasting only a few minutes as the Moon's shadow moves; produces the **'diamond ring' effect** just as the Moon begins to uncover the Sun).
        - **Partial Solar Eclipse:** Moon covers only a part of the Sun's disc.
      - **Eye Safety:** **Never look at a solar eclipse directly with the naked eye, sunglasses, binoculars, or telescopes** (intense radiation causes permanent blindness); view only through specialised **solar eclipse goggles** or by **projecting the Sun's image onto a screen** using a mirror on a ball or pinhole.
    - **12.3.2 Lunar Eclipse ($\text{Sun} \to \text{Earth} \to \text{Moon}$):**
      - Occurs on a Full Moon night when the **Earth comes directly between the Sun and the Moon**, blocking sunlight and casting the **Earth's shadow on the Moon**:
        - **Total Lunar Eclipse:** The entire full Moon enters Earth's shadow and appears **dark red** in colour.
        - **Partial Lunar Eclipse:** Only part of the Moon enters Earth's shadow.
      - **Safety & Coverage:** Completely **safe to view with the naked eye**, and visible across a **large part of the Earth** (unlike a total solar eclipse, which is visible only from a small shadow track).
